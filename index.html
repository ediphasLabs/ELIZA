About ELIZA

In 1966, Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT published a short program that could hold a conversation. Running a script called DOCTOR, it played a Rogerian psychotherapist, reflecting your words back as questions.

People confided in it. Weizenbaum's own secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it in private. That reaction troubled him for the rest of his life, and it gave us a name for the tendency to read understanding into a machine: the ELIZA effect.

How it works

  1. Tokenise & substitute. Your sentence is lower-cased, split into words, and normalised: dont → don't, i'm → i am, maybe → perhaps.
  2. Find a clause. The input is cut at punctuation and the word but. The first clause with a keyword is kept, and the rest is ignored.
  3. Rank keywords. Every keyword has a rank. computer (50) beats alike (10), which beats my (2). The highest is tried first.
  4. Decompose. Each keyword has patterns like * i am * @sad *. The first that fits splits your sentence into numbered pieces.
  5. Reassemble. A template such as I am sorry to hear that you are (3). is filled in, with pronouns flipped: my → your, me → you. Templates rotate so it doesn't repeat itself.
  6. Remember. Sentences about my … are quietly stored. When nothing else matches, ELIZA brings one back: "Earlier you said your …"

Open the trace on any reply to see each of these steps for that exact message.
